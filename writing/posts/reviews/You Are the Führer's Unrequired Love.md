# The nature of non-fiction 

## Narratives 

The book is structured in a way that treats Albert Speer and Hitler's relationship as a romantic one. At points the book touches on Hitler's presumed homosexuality, but it doesn't dwell on it. It gets dismissed, along with other attempts to explain away his behaviour - his distant Jewishness, his rejection from art school, his hermaphroditeness, his ugliness, ... all the things we know and use to charicature and psychologise him - they get dismissed as a *does it really matter?* It does, of course, depending on the story you are trying to tell and the narrative you are trying to craft, but this comes into the heart of what the book is about. Narratives and where they are really relevant. It is a self aware book, it itself is casting the relationship between Adolf Hitler and Albert Speer into a narrative of almost-homosexuality but definite homoeroticism and weird tension. Directly, the book is split into parts that mimic the stages of a relaitonship. When hitler dies, Speer is called the widower. The point is that there are narratives and we should be aware of them, and also the author of them and their intentions. I don't know if this book attempts to criticize the fact that anyone can look for a reason of anything etc.
